02 — Divisions

One community, two schools of thought

In the 14th century, a single question about grace and effort split the Sri Vaishnava community in two. The divide is still visible today — even on the forehead.

The 14th-Century Split

Centuries after Ramanujacharya's death, his followers began to disagree over a subtle but consequential question raised by the doctrine of prapatti: if salvation comes through Vishnu's grace, what role — if any — does human effort still play? The disagreement hardened into two distinct sub-sects, a divide often illustrated with a single memorable image.

"The Markata Nyaya, or 'monkey" school, holds the devotee clings to the divine the way a baby monkey clings to its mother. The Marjara Nyaya, or 'cat' school, holds the devotee is carried entirely, the way a mother cat carries her kitten by the scruff of its neck."

Vadakalai — The Northern School

Vadakalai, meaning roughly "the Northern branch," prioritizes the Sanskrit scriptural tradition and holds that human effort, exercised in partnership with divine grace, contributes meaningfully to salvation — the "monkey" position. Vadakalai theology follows the teachings of Vedanta Desika, a 13th–14th century philosopher, poet, and one of the tradition's most prolific commentators.

Tenkalai — The Southern School

Tenkalai, "the Southern branch," takes the opposite position: salvation is granted solely through divine grace, unconditionally and without regard to individual effort — the "cat" position. Tenkalai theology follows Manavala Mamuni and Pillai Lokacharya, and places heavier emphasis on the Tamil hymns of the Nalayira Divya Prabandham relative to Sanskrit texts.

What the Divide Looks Like Today

The clearest visible marker of the split is the tiruman — the U-shaped sacred mark worn on the forehead, made with white sandalwood or clay and a streak of red sri choornam. Vadakalai and Tenkalai families draw slightly different variations of this mark, distinguishable to anyone raised in the tradition at a glance. Temple affiliation, specific ritual sequencing, and even some naming conventions can also differ by sect — but the two communities share the same core devotional life, the same scripture, and often the same dinner table.
