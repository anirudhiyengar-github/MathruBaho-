01 — Foundations

Qualified non-dualism, and a soul held by grace

Every spice tin, every rite, every note of music in this culture rests on a philosophical foundation laid down nearly a thousand years ago. Start here.

Origins in Sri Vaishnavism

Iyengars are a community of Tamil-origin Hindu Brahmins who follow the Sri Vaishnava tradition, a school of thought centered on the exclusive worship of Vishnu. The tradition was systematized in the 11th and 12th centuries by the philosopher-saint Ramanujacharya, whose teachings still shape how the community understands devotion, scripture, and daily life today.

Unlike traditions that treat philosophy and ritual as separate tracks, Sri Vaishnavism folds the two together — a meal, a chant, a way of dressing, and a metaphysical claim about the soul are all understood as expressions of the same underlying belief system.

Core Belief: Visishtadvaita

The philosophical system underpinning Iyengar belief is called Visishtadvaita, often translated as "qualified non-dualism." It holds that the individual soul and the material world are both real — not illusions to be dissolved — but that both are entirely dependent on, and inseparable from, Vishnu. This is a middle path between strict dualism (which sees soul and divine as wholly separate) and strict non-dualism (which collapses the distinction between them entirely).

"The individual soul and the material world are real, but dependent — like a wave that is truly water, and yet never separate from the ocean."

Prapatti — Self-Surrender

Where many traditions emphasize personal effort as the path to liberation, Sri Vaishnavism places enormous weight on prapatti — complete, trusting self-surrender to Vishnu. Salvation, in this view, is ultimately an act of grace rather than achievement. This single idea becomes a fault line later in the community's history — the question of how much human effort matters alongside grace is exactly what splits the Iyengars into two sects, which the next stop on this journey covers.

The Twin Scriptures

The tradition treats two very different bodies of scripture as equally authoritative:

SanskritPrasthana Thrayi

The classical triad of Sanskrit philosophical texts shared across most Hindu traditions.

TamilNalayira Divya Prabandham

4,000 Tamil hymns composed by the Alvars, devotional poet-saints, held as scripture in their own right.

Granting a vernacular Tamil text the same authority as Sanskrit scripture was, historically, a distinctive and somewhat radical move — one that placed devotional poetry on equal footing with formal philosophy, and helped keep the tradition rooted in the Tamil-speaking world even as it engaged with pan-Indian ideas.
